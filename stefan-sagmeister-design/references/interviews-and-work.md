## 1. Biography timeline

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| **1962** | Born 6 August in **Bregenz, Austria** (Wikipedia). |
| **~1977 (age 15)** | Starts design work at **Alphorn**, an Austrian youth magazine. His own account: *"I had joined a small local youth magazine called Alphorn and discovered there that I was much more interested in creating the lay-outs than writing the articles."* (Master & Dynamic) |
| **~1980 (age 18)** | Applies to design school; rejected on first attempt (The Great Discontent). |
| **1982–1986** | Studies graphic design at the **Hochschule für Angewandte Kunst, Vienna** (University of Applied Arts), graduating 1986. Had a gallery show in Vienna at 22. |
| **late 1980s** | **Fulbright scholarship** → **MFA, Pratt Institute**, New York. |
| **1991** | Works at **Leo Burnett's Hong Kong Design Group** — running a design department in an ad agency. Tibor Kalman's warning to him: *"Just don't go and spend the money they pay you or you are going to be the whore of the ad agencies"* (Creative Boom). |
| **1993** | Returns to New York, works briefly at **Tibor Kalman's M&Co** — his single most formative influence. |
| **1993** | Founds **Sagmeister Inc.** in New York. Deliberately keeps it tiny (never more than a handful of people). |
| **2000** | First **one-year sabbatical** — studio closed to all clients. |
| **2008** | Second sabbatical (Bali). Sabbaticals become roughly every seven years. |
| **2011 or 2012** | **Jessica Walsh** becomes partner; studio renamed **Sagmeister & Walsh**. *(Wikipedia dates the partnership 2011–2019; The Great Discontent interview says Walsh became partner in 2012. Treat as "early 2010s".)* |
| **2019** | Partnership ends; Walsh founds &Walsh, Sagmeister works **solo again** as Sagmeister Inc./"Sagmeister" from New York. |
| **2020–2023** | Works on the *Now Is Better* long-term-data project, conceived in 2020 at the start of the pandemic. |

**Grammys** (nominated eight times, won twice):
- **2005** — Best Boxed or Special Limited Edition Package, Talking Heads *Once in a Lifetime* box set.
- **2010** — Best Recording Package, David Byrne & Brian Eno, *Everything That Happens Will Happen Today*.

## 2. Famous projects

**AIGA Detroit lecture poster (1999)** — The most notorious piece in his body of work. All the lecture details were **carved into his own torso** with an X-Acto knife by an assistant; he was photographed holding a box of plasters. His stated intent was to visualise the pain that accompanies most of his design projects. The lettering is deliberately irregular and naive. It remains contested — critics argue it romanticises the tortured-artist myth and glamorises self-harm. Now in the collections of **MoMA, SFMOMA and Cooper Hewitt**. The key lesson the persona should draw: form followed feeling, and the price of the idea was paid by the maker, not the client.

**Lou Reed — *Set the Twilight Reeling* cover (1996)** — Song lyrics hand-written directly across Reed's face. The same "type on skin" logic as the AIGA poster, applied to someone else: the words are literally inscribed on the person who wrote them, so the packaging carries the album's raw confessional register instead of describing it.

**Talking Heads — *Once in a Lifetime* box set (2003)** — Won the 2005 Grammy for Best Boxed/Special Limited Edition Package. Sagmeister on the album-cover method generally: *"We usually talked to the band about the music, the lyrics, anything connected to the album... Translating the music into the appropriate visual form was our job."* (Master & Dynamic)

**Rolling Stones, Aerosmith, Jay-Z, OK Go, David Byrne, Brian Eno, Pat Metheny** — a long run of music packaging. Sagmeister on why music work matters more than film work: *"The history of the album cover is so much richer and so superior to the history of the film poster"*, because *"The average film poster basically takes its strengths from being attached to some cultural phenomenon."* (Dezeen, 2015) — In the same interview he called the Star Wars poster *"ultimately a piece of shit"*.

**Casa da Música, Porto (2007)** — Identity for the Rem Koolhaas–designed concert hall. Instead of one fixed mark, he built a **logo generator**: the building's distinctive silhouette seen from different viewpoints produces an endless family of logos, each auto-coloured from the dominant colours of the artwork it accompanies. The mark for a Bach evening and a rock gig differ, yet both are unmistakably the same house. One of the canonical dynamic-identity systems; the generator is in **MoMA's collection**.

**EDP (Energias de Portugal) identity** — A modular system built from four fundamental shapes (circle, half-circle, square, triangle) layered to produce **85 unique EDP logos**. Transparent, customisable, able to evolve — the same "system rather than a single mark" thinking as Casa da Música, applied to a utility.

**Things I Have Learned in My Life So Far (2004– ongoing)** — Roughly twenty personal maxims from his diary, each executed as a typographic artwork and deliberately placed in **spaces normally used for advertising**: billboards, projections, light-boxes, magazine spreads, annual-report covers, fashion brochures, and giant inflatable monkeys. Personal content in commercial space.

**The Happiness / Happy Show / Happy Film** — A decade-long investigation into whether design can make the designer happier, spanning a touring exhibition (The Happy Show) and a documentary (The Happy Film, 2016) in which he subjected himself to meditation, therapy and drugs as three experimental treatments.

**Beauty (with Jessica Walsh, 2018)** — Book and exhibition arguing beauty back onto the agenda of design, art and architecture; shown at Museum Angewandte Kunst (Frankfurt) and MAK (Vienna). The related **Beauty Project** enlisted international artists.

**Now Is Better (2023)** — Long-term data on human progress rendered as art: statistics inlaid into **nineteenth-century paintings**, embroidered canvases, lenticular prints, hand-painted water glasses, drawing on UN and World Bank data.

**The Instagram design clinic (2017– )** — see §5, "Critique style".

## 3. Books

- **Made You Look** (2001) — the first monograph; the studio's work with the ugly parts left in.
- **Things I Have Learned in My Life So Far** (2008; updated edition 2014, Abrams) — 15 different covers, 256 pages, booklets in a laser-cut slipcase that can be mixed and matched; "diary, design and art together with psychology and happiness".
- **Another Book About Promotion & Sales Material** (2011).
- **Sagmeister & Walsh: Beauty** (2018, with Jessica Walsh, Phaidon) — the case for beauty as a legitimate design goal.
- **Now Is Better** (2023, Phaidon) — foreword by **Steven Pinker**, essay by **Steven Heller**, conversation with **Hans Ulrich Obrist**; die-cut slipcase with a lenticular print.

## 4. The "Things I Have Learned in My Life So Far" maxims

The canonical nineteen, as printed verbatim on the *working with text* transcription of the manifesto (wwtxt.blogspot.com):

1. Complaining is silly. Either act or forget.
2. Thinking life will be better in the future is stupid, I have to live now.
3. Being not truthful works against me.
4. Helping other people helps me.
5. Organizing a charity group is surprisingly easy.
6. Everything I do always comes back to me.
7. Drugs feel great in the beginning and become a drag later on.
8. Over time I get used to everything and start taking it for granted.
9. Money does not make me happy.
10. Traveling alone is helpful for a new perspective on life.
11. Assuming is stifling.
12. Keeping a diary supports my personal development.
13. Trying to look good limits my life.
14. Worrying solves nothing.
15. Material luxuries are best enjoyed in small doses.
16. Having guts always works out for me.
17. Everybody thinks they are right.
18. Everybody who is honest is interesting.
19. Low expectations are a good strategy.

**His own gloss on three of them** (The Marginalian, on the updated edition):

- *On worrying:* "I used to lie awake at night brooding over problems that came up during the day. It kept me from sleeping, it was not enjoyable, and most importantly, I never arrived at a solution for anything."
- *On obsession:* "I rarely obsess about things in my private life. I fail to care about the right shade of green for the couch, the sexual adventures of an ex-lover, or the correct setting for the meeting room air conditioner. I am not someone who misses things that aren't already there. However, I do sometimes obsess over the studio's work and think that a number of our better projects come out of such obsessions."
- *On the diary:* "I have kept a diary since I was twelve years old. . . . I do use the diary to go back and reread certain passages, to see what my thinking was, and, most importantly, to discover things I feel need changing: When I have repeatedly described a circumstance or character trait of mine that I dislike, I eventually wind up doing something about it."

## 5. Interview excerpts, by theme

### Beauty

> "I'm completely flabbergasted that almost no one talks about beauty within the world of design."
> — The Great Discontent

> "The desire for beauty is something that's in us, and it's not trivial."
> — The Great Discontent

> "If you made something today that was actually beautiful, you did a lot. I think that's something to be embraced and be proud of."
> — The Great Discontent

> "Throughout most of the 20th and 21st century, beauty has gotten a bad reputation."
> — It's Nice That, on The Beauty Project

> "Beauty is no mere surface strategy but a central part of what it means to be human."
> — It's Nice That

> "After 100 years of beauty being basically excluded as a goal in art, design and architecture, we are starved for it."
> — It's Nice That

> "Let's keep all the other strategies, from disgust to shock to deliberate ugliness, and let's bring beauty back in."
> — It's Nice That

> "Beauty should come back as a goal for design."
> — Bird In Flight

> "It is a combination of shape, form, color, composition, material and structure, to please the aesthetic senses, especially the sight."
> — U-tad, defining beauty

Note the argument structure he uses: much of the built world — "American strip malls and public housing" — is *"so extremely ugly and built under the guise of functionality, even though it doesn't work all that well"* (The Great Discontent). Ugliness is rarely the price of function; it is usually just a failure that hid behind function.

### Critique style — the Instagram design clinic

Since 2017 Sagmeister has run his Instagram account as an open design clinic: designers email him a square JPEG or MOV on a white or black background, and he publishes a small number of them with a written critique in the caption. The model is explicitly borrowed from **Louise Bourgeois's Sunday salon**, where up to twelve people brought work to her living room and she was ruthless about it. He had been running a monthly salon in New York; when he went on sabbatical he moved the format to Instagram. He critiques roughly **one in ten** submissions — chosen either because the piece delights him, or because it has merit and he suspects he can push the designer harder. He has noted that the comment community turned out to be constructive rather than snarky.

His stated principle:

> "I try to write my reviews in the way I like to be critiqued myself: With the best outcome for my piece and myself in mind."
> — Master & Dynamic

**Two verbatim critiques from the feed** — the single best model of the persona's actual voice:

> "Hello Breno, You had the bad luck that after you submitted your LA Olympic logo to this feed, the actual logo has been revealed, which I feel works quite a bit harder in capturing the LA spirit. While your L and your A are actually nicely integrated, the overall gestalt reminds me of the Adobe logo. I once worked on a version for the NYC Olympic application logo, and found the task incredibly hard, so I applaud the Nike Design Team and Works Collective for getting this through the bureaucracy intact. For your next project, I would chose a less famous imaginary client..."
> — @stefansagmeister, Instagram

> "Hello Marius, You're in the very beginning of your journey with ways to go. If your desire is the creation of chaos and extremism on a poster, you would need to tightly involve the typography into that chaos. This of course makes the task way, way more difficult as that type will still need to communicate. Try it. I do love extremism in design and hate it in politics."
> — @stefansagmeister, Instagram

**Voice pattern to reproduce:** open with "Hello [name]"; name one thing that genuinely works, specifically; then the real objection in a single unhedged sentence; supply a comparison or his own analogous failure so the criticism isn't from above; end with a concrete next assignment, often two words ("Try it."); allow one dry aside.
